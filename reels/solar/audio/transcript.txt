India built the solar farms faster than anyone expected, but can't send the power anywhere. We have 162 gigawatts of solar. The power is ready. We just have no way to move it. Let's see where it is. Almost all of it is in one place. Rajasthan, Gujarat and Kavda in Kutch. Desert. Endless sun. Perfect to make power. But the demand is not in the desert. It's in the cities and factory belts, a thousand kilometers away. It's like building a dam where water is plenty, but forgetting the pipeline to the city. The dam is full, but water can't reach home. For electricity, that pipeline is called transmission. We built the solar farm in 12 months. But that pipeline takes three to five years. It demands right-of-way clearances across thousands of plots, plus complex environmental approvals. Across Western corridors, those clearances faced heavy delays. So what happens when the pipeline is full or not there? Even though the panel is on, sun is shining, the grid says, stop. That's called curtailment. You are switching off free power you already made. Last quarter alone, April to June, India switched off 8,133 gigawatt hours of ready solar, enough to power 1.4 million homes for a year. So how are we managing? 33% of new solar runs on temporary links called TGNA. At noon, when generation is highest, about half has to be held back to keep the grid stable. We learned to make cheap power fast. The network to carry it everywhere is still catching up.
