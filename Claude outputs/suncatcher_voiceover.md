# Google is sending its AI chips to space — Voiceover

**Length:** about 230 words, roughly 85–90 seconds.

---

Next week, Google is launching its AI chips into space.
Not to study space. To run AI up there.

Here's why.
Every time you ask an AI something, a data centre somewhere burns electricity.
By 2030, data centres could use as much power as all of Japan.
And on Earth, power is running out. Towns are already fighting new data centres over electricity and water.

So Google asked a strange question: what if the data centre wasn't on Earth at all?

In the right orbit, a satellite sits in almost constant sunlight.
Its solar panels make up to eight times more power than on the ground.
No night. No clouds. No electricity bill.

On October 1st, a SpaceX rocket will carry a small satellite built by Planet, with four Google TPUs inside, the same chips that run Gemini.
The test is simple: can they survive?

Because space is brutal.
Radiation can flip bits inside the chip.
There's no air, so heat can't blow away. It has to glow out through radiators.
And if something breaks, nobody can go up and fix it.

If it works, the plan gets huge: 81 satellites flying just a few hundred metres apart, talking through lasers, working like one giant computer.

But there's a catch. It only makes sense when rockets get about ten times cheaper.

And here's the twist. The rocket Google is riding belongs to its biggest rival in this race.
SpaceX wants to launch up to a million AI satellites of its own.

So the next AI war might not be fought on Earth.
It might be won by whoever owns the sky.

---

**Fact notes (for on-screen text):**

- **The launch:** Project Suncatcher's first satellite goes up on 1 Oct 2026, on SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare (Falcon 9, Vandenberg).
  - The satellite is built by Planet and carries 4 Trillium TPUs.
  - It will fly in low Earth orbit at about 650 km.
- **Sundar Pichai's tweet:** "Can our TPUs survive and operate in space? … One small step for TPUs."
- **Power demand:** the IEA expects data-centre electricity to reach about 945 TWh by 2030, roughly Japan's total use today.
- **Sunlight:** in a dawn–dusk sun-synchronous orbit the satellite gets near-constant sun, and its solar panels produce up to 8× more power than on the ground.
- **Ground tests so far:**
  - Radiation: a 67 MeV proton beam, more than 5 years of space radiation, with no hard failures. The memory (HBM) showed some errors, which Google calls acceptable for running AI but a concern for training it.
  - Launch shaking: the chips survived 50–100 g.
- **Cooling:** there's no air in space, so heat pipes carry it to radiators.
- **The bigger plan:** clusters of 81 satellites within a 1 km radius, about 650 km up, linked by lasers that need about 10 Tbps each (today's satellite links manage 1–100 Gbps). Two satellites test the laser links in 2027.
- **Cost:** launching costs about $1,500–2,900 per kg today. It makes sense at about $200/kg, possibly by the mid-2030s with Starship.
- **SpaceX's own plan:**
  - In January 2026 it asked the FCC for permission to launch up to 1 million orbital data-centre satellites.
  - It merged with xAI in February 2026.
  - Musk: "within a few years the lowest cost to generate AI compute will be space."
