# India's own GPS is quietly failing — Voiceover

**Length:** about 225 words, roughly 85–90 seconds at a natural pace.

---

India spent over two thousand crore rupees building its own GPS.
And right now… it can't tell you where you are.

To understand why, go back to 1999, to the Kargil war.
Indian forces needed GPS data from the region. The US, which runs GPS, refused.
India realised that in a war, someone else could switch off our maps.

So ISRO built its own system. It's called NavIC.

Here's how it works.
Your phone listens to signals from satellites in space.
Each signal carries the exact time it was sent.
Your phone measures how long each one took to arrive, and from that it works out how far away each satellite is.
With four satellites, it can pin down your location.

But this only works if the time is perfect.
Off by just one billionth of a second, and your location shifts by thirty centimetres.
That's why every satellite carries atomic clocks.

And that's where it went wrong.
NavIC's clocks were imported, and one after another, they started dying in space.
One replacement satellite never reached orbit.
In January 2025, another got stuck in the wrong orbit because a valve didn't open.

Today, only three satellites are working.
Remember, you need four.

So what now?
In October, ISRO plans to launch a new satellite, and this time it carries an atomic clock made in India.

If it works, India gets its maps back.
If it doesn't… we're still borrowing someone else's.

---

**Fact notes (for on-screen text):**

- **Kargil, 1999:** the US denied India GPS data for the region.
- **Cost:** about ₹2,246 crore spent by 2017.
- **Launches:** 11 NavIC satellites since 2013, against a planned constellation of 7.
- **Accuracy target:** about 3 m.
- **Clock failures:** the first failures were on IRNSS-1A in 2016. By July 2025, 5 satellites had lost all their clocks.
- **Lost satellites:**
  - IRNSS-1H (2017): the heat shield didn't separate, so it never reached orbit.
  - NVS-02 (29 Jan 2025): ISRO's 100th launch from Sriharikota. A pyro valve failed, the engine couldn't fire, and it was stranded in transfer orbit.
  - IRNSS-1F: its last clock failed on 13 Mar 2026.
- **Working today:** 3 satellites (IRNSS-1B, IRNSS-1L, NVS-01). That's below the 4 needed for a position fix; the timing service still works.
- **NVS-03:** GSLV Mk II, launch window 15–20 Oct 2026. It carries an Indian rubidium atomic clock built by SAC Ahmedabad. NVS-04 and NVS-05 are to follow.
- **Timing maths:** 1 nanosecond × the speed of light ≈ 30 cm.
